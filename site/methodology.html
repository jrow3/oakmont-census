The short version

Oakmont isn't its own city or town in the eyes of the U.S. Census Bureau, so there's no ready-made "Oakmont" in the data. To draw the community accurately, we trace it from 75 individual census blocks that together match Oakmont's boundary.

From there, two different government datasets fill in the picture, and they don't work the same way. One is an exact head-count. The other is a survey that produces estimates. Things like how many people live here, how old they are, and who owns versus rents come from the exact count. Other things, like income, education, and home values, only exist as survey estimates for a larger area.

That's the whole story in one line: exact counts where we can get them, careful estimates where we can't. The rest of this page explains why, in as much or as little detail as you want.

The two sources

Two kinds of Census data

Almost every number here comes from one of two U.S. Census Bureau programs. Knowing which is which explains everything else.

The Decennial Census

Exact · counts only · reaches the block

A 100% head-count taken every ten years (2020 is the latest). Because it counts everyone, it's exact, with no margin of error, and it's published all the way down to a single city block. But it asks only a handful of questions, so it gives us counts: population, age, sex, race, and whether homes are owned, rented, or vacant.

The American Community Survey

Estimated · rich detail · stops at the tract

A rolling survey sent to a sample of households, published as five-year averages. Because it's a sample, its numbers are estimates with a margin of error, and its detailed tables are only published for larger areas. But it asks far more: income, education, home value, marital status, and where people were born. It's where all the richer detail comes from.

The trade-off, in a phrase: exactness versus richness, and geography versus detail. The next section is the geography half.

The exact Decennial count reaches the block, so we can trace Oakmont's 75 blocks precisely. The ACS's detailed tables stop at the tract, a larger area of a few thousand people. Oakmont sits inside Census Tracts 1516.01 and 1516.02, which also take in some non-Oakmont residents along the edges.

Both maps are at the same scale. The tracts (left) are the smallest area the ACS survey reports, and they reach past Oakmont; the 75 Decennial blocks (right) trace the community itself. That gap is exactly why counts use the blocks and estimates use the tracts.

There's one more hurdle. Oakmont has no Census "place" code (those are reserved for incorporated cities and recognized communities), and there's no machine-readable map of the Oakmont Village Association boundary to hand the Census API. So the exact view isn't a built-in geography; it's a hand-picked list of the 75 blocks that best trace the community's edge.

A common question

Two places inside the line

Two parts of Oakmont come up often enough to deserve a straight answer: Wild Oak, which isn't age-restricted, and Oakmont Gardens, the senior-living community on White Oak Drive. Both sit inside the boundary these figures count. Neither can be separated out — and it turns out that matters far less than you'd expect.

Why they can't be separated

The Decennial count reaches the block, and that's the end of the line — the Census publishes nothing smaller. Both of these areas are smaller than the block they sit in, and they share that block with ordinary Oakmont homes. Removing the block would take the neighbours with it.

Wild Oak is 41 addresses in a block that holds 263 — dropping it would delete 222 Oakmont homes to remove 41. Oakmont Gardens is 169 addresses in a block of 281, alongside 112 other homes. Splitting a block by hand would mean inventing the split, which is exactly the shortcut this project avoids.

Oakmont Gardens is the only multi-unit property of any size in the community — the next largest address holds six homes. Because its residents are overwhelmingly older, setting them aside would nudge the age figures down, not up, and only by a fraction of a point either way.

Tenure is the one place it genuinely matters. Every home at Oakmont Gardens is rented, which makes it roughly 24% of all 707 rented homes in Oakmont. Set it aside and the owner-occupied share rises from 77.9% to about 82.2%. That is the single largest effect either area has on any published number, and it is worth knowing when you read the ownership figures.

How these were measured

Census data can't size either area, so this uses Sonoma County's public address-point file, counting the addresses on Wild Oak Drive and Court, and the unit addresses at 301 White Oak Drive. As a check on the method, those address points total 3,524 across the footprint against 3,427 Census housing units — close enough agreement to trust the shares. The population figures are ranges rather than single numbers, because the split of a block's residents between an area and its neighbours isn't published; the range spans two reasonable ways of estimating it.

Worth keeping in proportion: every income, education and home-value figure on this site is a tract estimate, and the tracts already reach past Oakmont to take in roughly 955 non-Oakmont residents along Los Alamos Road, Bennett Valley and the Highway 12 corridor. That is many times either area discussed here, and no change to the block list would affect it.

Why the income figures cover more than Oakmont

Income, education, home value and household size are ACS survey estimates, and the ACS stops at the tract. Oakmont's two tracts reach past the community to take in roughly 955 non-Oakmont residents — the rural Highway 12 corridor, Los Alamos Road, Bennett Valley and the Bennett Ridge hillsides. Those households are younger and differently housed, so tract-based shares read slightly lower than an Oakmont-only count would. There is no way to correct this: block-level ACS data does not exist.

Household or family?

A household is everyone living in one home, including a person living alone. A family is a householder plus relatives. The family median runs highest; the household median sits below it because it also counts Oakmont's many one-person homes. Neither is wrong — they answer different questions.

What the Census counts as income

The Census measures money income: cash a household receives, counted before taxes. It does include Social Security, SSI, pensions and regular retirement-account withdrawals (IRA, Roth IRA, 401(k)), VA payments, and interest and dividends — whether or not any of it is taxable. It does not include proceeds from selling a home, withdrawals from savings, money borrowed against a home (reverse mortgage, HELOC), inheritances, life insurance payouts, or one-time gifts.

That gap matters in a retirement community: a household living comfortably on savings and home equity can still report a modest income here.

Why amounts sometimes read "not published" or "not disclosed"

The sources-of-income table shows an average per receiving household, because the Census publishes no median by income source at any geography — only totals, from which an average can be derived. Averages are pulled upward by a few large values, so read them as a rough centre rather than a typical household.

Where no amount appears, the reason differs and the wording says which: not published means the Census collects no dollar figure for that source anywhere (SNAP benefits, for example); not disclosed means the figure exists but too few households reported it to publish without identifying them; and a dash means no household here reports that source at all.

A note on marital status

In 2020 the federal ACS did not consistently record same-sex married couples as married, which understates marriage among Oakmont's same-sex couples.

For the data-minded

The fine print

Why not just get ACS data for the 75 blocks?

This is the most common question, and the answer is that block-level ACS demographics don't exist. Because the ACS is a sample, publishing its estimates for a tiny area would be both statistically unreliable and a privacy risk, so the Census Bureau only releases its detailed tables for areas of roughly 1,200 people or more. Income, education, and home value for a single block are never published, at any margin of error. Approaches that appear to show block-level ACS demographics are really filling the gap with non-Census sources such as member surveys or commercial address data; those aren't comparable to Census figures, so this site doesn't use them. Excluding blocks to trace a boundary is legitimate only for the Decennial count, which is exactly where we apply it.

Differential privacy

The 2020 Decennial Census adds a small amount of statistical noise to block-level counts to protect individuals (the Bureau's "differential privacy" system). For a single block this can nudge a number by a few people; summed across all 75 Oakmont blocks it largely averages out, which is why the exact-boundary totals are dependable even though any one block isn't guaranteed to the person.

Margins of error

Every ACS figure is an estimate with a published margin of error. We show the point estimates for readability, so read them as "about," not "exactly." The five-year 2020 ACS also overlaps in time with the 2016–2020 and 2020–2024 windows, which is why the 2024 comparison page calls its year-over-year changes "directional, not precise."

What the Census counts as income

The income figures on this site measure what the Census Bureau calls money income: cash a household actually receives over twelve months, counted before taxes. It is not a measure of taxable income, and the definition draws no line between taxable and non-taxable money.

Counted: wages and salary; self-employment income; interest, dividends, rent, royalties and income from estates or trusts (interest counts whether or not it is tax-exempt, so municipal bond interest is in); Social Security and Railroad Retirement; Supplemental Security Income (SSI); public assistance; retirement, survivor and disability pensions, including regular withdrawals from an IRA, Roth IRA, 401(k) or 403(b); and "all other income," which covers Department of Veterans Affairs payments, unemployment and workers' compensation, alimony and child support, and regular contributions from people outside the household.

Not counted: capital gains; money from selling property, including a home; withdrawals from savings; money borrowed, which is where reverse mortgages and home equity lines fall; tax refunds; one-time gifts and lump-sum inheritances; life insurance and other lump-sum payouts; and the value of non-cash benefits such as SNAP, housing subsidies or employer-paid health insurance.

That distinction matters more in Oakmont than in most places. A retired household drawing down savings, or borrowing against a home it owns outright, can be living comfortably and still report a modest income in these tables. Read the income figures as cash received, not as a measure of wealth or of what a household can afford. The definitions above are the Census Bureau's own, from the ACS Subject Definitions, "Income in the Past 12 Months."

The variables, by table

Every figure traces to a public Census table. The main ones:

  • P12 age & sex (Decennial). The site reports the 55+ share (total minus the under-55 bands) because Oakmont is a 55+ community.
  • P3 race, P4 Hispanic origin (Decennial).
  • H1 housing units, H3 occupancy, H4 tenure / owner vs. renter (Decennial).
  • B19013 household income, B19301 per-capita income (ACS).
  • B15001 educational attainment, ages 45+ (ACS).
  • B25077 median home value (ACS), owner-occupied homes only.

All of these are public. You can pull them yourself from the Census API, or use this site's Full Data Explorer on the 2020 Portrait page, which mirrors every published table for these geographies.
